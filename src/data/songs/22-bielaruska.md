# Апісаньне / Description

## be

**«Беларуска»** — песьня на верш Ніны Аксёнчык «Як умее кахаць беларуска». Гэта ода беларускай жанчыне і яе каханьню: яна ўмее любіць, умее чакаць, умее кахаць — і носіць гэтае каханьне з сабою праз слату і гады, нягледзячы ні на што.

::: cut Пра аўтарку
Ніна Аксёнчык нарадзілася 12 сакавіка 1961 году ў вёсцы Вятчын Жыткавіцкага раёну, на Палесьсі. Скончыла Мазырскі педагагічны інстытут (1982) і асьпірантуру пры кафедры беларускай літаратуры Мінскага педінстытуту. У 1988-м абараніла кандыдацкую дысертацыю, да 1997 году выкладала ў Мазыры.

Музычна гэта цёмны, цырыманіяльны deep dubstep з фолкавымі каранямі: шэптаны рытуальны вакал, самотная віяланчэль, глыбокі саб-бас і халфтайм-удар, якія ў прыпеве выбухаюць эпічным сьпевам-заклінаньнем.

## en

**«Biełaruska»** ("A Belarusian Woman") is a song set to Nina Aksionchyk's poem "How a Belarusian Woman Loves." It is an ode to the Belarusian woman and her love: she knows how to love, how to wait, how to be in love — and she carries that love with her through foul weather and years, no matter what.

::: cut About the author
Nina Aksionchyk was born on 12 March 1961 in the village of Viatchyn, Žytkavičy district, in the Palessie region. She graduated from the Mazyr Pedagogical Institute (1982) and did postgraduate studies in Belarusian literature at the Minsk Pedagogical Institute, defended her candidate's dissertation in 1988, and taught in Mazyr until 1997. A poet, critic, and amateur artist, she was published in the collective anthology *Paleskija pierazvony* (1994); her first book of her own was [*Spoviedź arabiny*](https://literature.mozyrlib.by/) ("A Rowan's Confession", 1995). A member of the Union of Belarusian Writers since 1996, she now lives in the Czech Republic.
:::

Musically this is dark, ceremonial deep dubstep with folk roots: whispered ritual vocals, a lone cello, deep sub-bass and a half-time kick that erupt in the chorus into an epic chanted incantation.

# Заўвагі да тэксту / Notes on the lyrics

## be

## en

**Notes for readers outside the Belarusian context**

- **The poem.** "How a Belarusian Woman Loves" (*Jak umieje kachać bielaruska*) by Nina Aksionchyk, a contemporary poet writing in the popular song-lyric vein — the refrain structure is that of a sung verse. The speaker is the *bielaruska*, the Belarusian woman, so the love poem doubles as a small national self-portrait.

- **Two verbs for love.** The refrain's climax is untranslatable: Belarusian distinguishes *liubić* — to love in general (people, places, things) — from *kachać*, to love romantically, said only of a beloved. The original ascends *liubić* → *čakać* ("to wait") → *kachać*, ending on the stronger, strictly romantic verb; English must make do with "love / wait / be in love."

- **The waiting woman.** "Knowing how to wait" is the core of the ideal invoked here: faithful waiting through separation is one of the oldest virtues of East Slavic love lyric — soldiers' and recruits' songs are built on it — and this poem inherits it, adding the Christian gesture of hallowing the road with prayer.

- **Haściniec ("high road").** An old word for a broad main road, related to *hość*, "guest" — the road guests travel by. The triad of high roads, highways, and a narrow footpath means simply: wherever life leads, on the great road or the small.

- **Halubić ("to caress").** Literally "to dove": the verb derives from *holub*, "dove," so the night moon dove-tenderly caresses the willows. The dove is the folk emblem of tenderness — a beloved is called *halubka*, "little dove."

- **Vierbaloz (willow thickets).** Osier willows lining rivers and wet meadows are stock scenery of the Belarusian lowland; moonlit willows are to Belarusian lyric roughly what nightingales and birches are elsewhere in the Slavic world.
