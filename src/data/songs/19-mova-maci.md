## en

“Mova-Mači” is a musical adaptation of a poem by Ryhor Baradulin, which the poet wrote especially for the opening of the “Mova Nanova” Belarusian language courses.

- **The poem.** "Nanova!" ("Anew!") by Ryhor Baradulin (1935–2014), written for the opening of the Mova Nanova language courses — one of the last poems by the man many consider the greatest Belarusian poet of the late twentieth century. He was the last to bear the official title People's Poet of Belarus, was nominated for the Nobel Prize, and died only weeks after the courses began. The text here follows a song arrangement, with the refrain repeated.

- **Mova Nanova ("Language Anew").** Free, volunteer-run Belarusian-language courses founded in 2014 by Hleb Labadzienka and Alesia Litvinoŭskaja, which grew into a network across a dozen cities and became the flagship of the language revival — thousands of adults re-learning the language they call native. After the post-2020 crackdown the courses, like most independent initiatives, could no longer operate openly inside Belarus and continue online and in exile. The capitalized "Anew" (Nanova) in the poem's triple refrain is a direct salute to their name.

- **Why courses in one's "own" language?** Belarusian is the language most citizens name as native, yet daily life, schooling and officialdom run overwhelmingly in Russian after two centuries of imperial and Soviet russification, and UNESCO lists Belarusian as vulnerable. Choosing to speak it is therefore a conscious act — hence an entire genre of Belarusian poetry devoted to the mova, of which this is a late, luminous example. In this discourse "the mova," used absolutely, means Belarusian and nothing else.

A whisper. An organ. A choir. Sub-bass. A dubstep drop. Drum and bass energy. Here, the Belarusian word sounds like a ritual in a cyber-cathedral — sacred, powerful, and alive. This is not gentle nostalgia, but a return at full volume. Inevitable. Healing. Anew.

# Заўвагі да тэксту / Notes on the lyrics

## be

## en

- **Hadavać ("to nurture").** The verb used for raising children and rearing living creatures: the language is cast not as a monument to be preserved but as a living being to be brought up.

- **The holy wellspring.** A krynica is a spring of water, and venerated "holy springs" with healing water are a living feature of Belarusian folk piety. Calling the mother tongue a holy wellspring — together with the "healing" (hajuča) of the opening line and the "headwaters" (vytoki) a few lines later — makes the whole poem run on water: language as the spring one returns to and is healed by.

- **Ščyry ("heartfelt").** Sincere, open-hearted, genuine — a key self-descriptive value word of Belarusian culture (a ščyry bielarus is a true-hearted Belarusian). The "ščyrае word" that unfolds is both an honest word and a distinctly Belarusian one.
