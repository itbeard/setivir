# Апісаньне / Description

## be

Гэтая песьня пераасэнсоўвае культавы верш Янкі Купалы *[«А хто там ідзе?»](https://be.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%90_%D1%85%D1%82%D0%BE_%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC_%D1%96%D0%B4%D0%B7%D0%B5%3F)* — твор пачатку 1900-х пра цяжкі лёс беларускага народу, у якім агромністая грамада ўпершыню на ўвесь голас кажа, чаго яна хоча: **«людзьмі звацца»**. Максім Горкі, пераклаўшы верш на расейскую, [прарочыў](https://proza.ru/2019/10/02/998), што ён «можа быць, на час стане народным гімнам беларусаў», — і прароцтва спраўдзілася літаральна: у сьнежні 1917 году, на адкрыцьці Ўсебеларускага зьезду ў Менску, хор [засьпяваў](https://www.svaboda.org/a/29053872.html) «А хто там ідзе?» менавіта як беларускі гімн. А сёньня гэты твор перакладзены ўжо больш чым на сотню моваў сьвету.

Ідэя стварэньня трэка прыйшла да мяне акурат пасьля прагляду фільма пра няпросты лёс самога Купалы — [«Купала»](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupa%C5%82a_%28film%29). Кіно гэтае ня толькі біяграфічнае: яно добра паказвае, хто такія беларусы і як нашыя выдатныя дзеячы змагаліся сто гадоў таму за нашую культуру, літаральна выцарапваючы яе з лап камуністычнай улады. Фільм з моцнай нацыянальнай ідэяй зьняла нашая дзяржаўная кінастудыя «Беларусьфільм» — і сама ж яго пахавала. Прэм'ера мелася адбыцца яшчэ ў 2019-м, але яе адклалі — як пішуць, з асьцярогі раззлаваць Маскву посткаланіяльным кіно, дзе Беларусь паказаная калоніяй спачатку Расейскай імпэрыі, а потым савецкай. Пасьля 2020-га стужку схавалі канчаткова: у 2021-м «Беларусьфільм» адклікаў яе з лонданскага фэстывалю за два дні да паказу. Такія часы. Аднак фільм можна паглядзець, напрыклад, [тут](https://kinakipa.site/movie?id=1252445) — вельмі раю.

Песьня **«А хто там ідзе?»** зробленая як жорсткая лічбавая атака: цяжар гістарычнай крыўды тут узмацняюць магутны бас і агрэсіўныя электрарытмы. Песьня-спадарожнік для беларусаў, якія абралі шлях змаганьня за волю.

## en

This song reimagines the iconic poem by Yanka Kupala, the national poet of Belarus — *«А хто там ідзе?»* (*A chto tam idzie?* — "And Who Goes There?"). Written in the early 1900s, it speaks of the hard lot of the Belarusian people, and in it, for the first time, an enormous multitude declares out loud what it wants: *"to be called human."* The Russian writer Maxim Gorky, who translated the poem, [prophesied](https://proza.ru/2019/10/02/998) that it "may, for a time, become the national anthem of the Belarusians" — and the prophecy came true quite literally: in December 1917, at the opening of the All-Belarusian Congress in Minsk, a choir [sang](https://www.svaboda.org/a/29053872.html) "A chto tam idzie?" precisely as the Belarusian anthem. (That Congress — the first great national assembly of Belarusians — was soon dispersed by the Bolsheviks; its delegates went on to proclaim Belarusian independence in 1918, the anniversary now marked as Freedom Day.) Today the poem has been translated into more than a hundred languages of the world.

The idea for this track came to me right after watching a film about the difficult fate of Kupala himself — "[Kupała](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupa%C5%82a_%28film%29)". It is more than a biopic: the film shows very well who Belarusians are, and how our great figures fought for our culture a hundred years ago — literally clawing it out of the paws of the communist regime. This film with a powerful national idea was made by our state film studio, Belarusfilm — which then buried it itself. The premiere was due back in 2019, but it was postponed — reportedly out of fear of angering Moscow with a post-colonial film in which Belarus appears as a colony, first of the Russian Empire and then of the Soviet one. After 2020 the film was shelved for good: in 2021 Belarusfilm withdrew it from a London festival two days before the screening. Such are the times. And yet the film, with English subtitles, can be watched, for example, [here](https://kinakipa.site/movie?id=1252445) — I highly recommend it.

The song **"A chto tam idzie?"** is built as a harsh digital assault: the weight of historical grievance is amplified here by powerful bass and aggressive electro-rhythms. A companion song for Belarusians who have chosen the path of the struggle for freedom.

# Заўвагі да тэксту / Notes on the lyrics

## be

## en

- **The translation.** The English translation of the poem is by [Vera Rich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_Rich) (1936–2009) — a London poet, journalist and translator who devoted more than half a century to Belarusian poetry. Her anthology "Like Water, Like Fire" (1971), published under the auspices of UNESCO, was the first collection of Belarusian verse ever to appear in a Western language, and she translated all 221 poems in it herself. Kupala, Kolas, Bahdanovič, Arsiennieva — whole generations of Belarusian poets speak English in her voice; her translation is reproduced here in tribute to her memory.

- **The form.** A question-and-answer structure, like a folk call-and-response or a catechism: an outside voice asks, and the marching crowd answers in terse replies. The poem stages the moment a nameless peasant mass names itself — "Belarusians" — before the world.

- **Kryŭda ("grievance").** The wrong one has suffered, an injustice borne. Carrying it "to show the whole world" means testimony rather than revenge — a demand that the wrong be recognized. It is the poem's central concept word.

- **Bast shoes (lapci).** Footwear woven from lime-tree bark, the mark of the poorest peasants. Belarusians were then an overwhelmingly peasant people; gaunt shoulders, bloodied hands and bast shoes compress that condition into a single line.

- **Hramada ("throng").** A great crowd, but also a community or assembly — and the name of the first Belarusian political organizations (the Belarusian Socialist Hramada was active exactly when the poem was written), so for contemporaries the word carried a political ring: the people gathering as one body.

- **"To be called people" (liudźmi zvacca).** The poem's last two words became the foundational formula of Belarusian self-assertion — a demand not for power or privilege but for elementary human and national dignity. Liudzi means both "people" and "human beings," so the phrase claims humanity and peoplehood in one breath; it has been quoted for over a century, up to the protest banners of 2020.
