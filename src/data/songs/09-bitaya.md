## be

Цэлы пласт беларускай гісторыі і народнай памяці зьвязаны з рэкруцтвам і вайсковай павіннасьцю. У даўнія часы хлопцы — хто з гонарам, а хто й не — ішлі бараніць бацькаўшчыну, а іхныя дзяўчаты заставаліся чакаць каханых дома. Нездарма рэкруцкія песьні сталі асобным жанрам нашага фальклёру. Менавіта пра такую гісторыю — расстаньне маладой пары — і пяецца ў беларускай народнай баладзе «Бітая дарожанька».

Гэтая песьня запала мне ў душу яшчэ з ракаўскіх народных сьпеваў. Яе выконвалі мае старэйшыя сябры з гурта «Гасьцінец» — дарослага ракаўскага фальклёрнага калектыву (у адрозьненьне ад дзіцячага «Вянка») пад кіраўніцтвам Елізаветы Станіславаўны Пятроўскай. Пасьпеў паўдзельнічаць у ім і я — ужо падлеткам і пераважна ў якасьці танцора (голас якраз ламаўся, дый часу на сьпевы не ставала: падлеткавы ўзрост, шмат справаў па-за хорам — ну вы разумееце, пра што я 😊).

::: cut Пра гурт «Гасьцінец»

А вось як пра гурт адгукнуўся мэтр беларускай народнай песьні, прафесар [Міхаіл Дрынеўскі](https://be.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%9C%D1%96%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%96%D0%BB_%D0%9F%D0%B0%D1%9E%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B2%D1%96%D1%87_%D0%94%D1%80%D1%8B%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%9E%D1%81%D0%BA%D1%96):

Так я на адзін вечар зноў вярнуўся ў «Гасьцінец»: танцаваць рыцарскі танец на галоўнай сцэне краіны.

Тады я яшчэ не да канца ўсьведамляў, хто такі «прэзідэнт» і да якіх наступстваў — для краіны і для мяне асабіста — ягонае «прэзідэнцтва» прывядзе ў 2020 годзе. Але з гісторыі, як і зь песьні, слова ня выкінеш: менавіта тое выкананьне «Бітай дарожанькі» — з доўгім і пранізьлівым уступным словам ад Елізаветы Станіславаўны — засталося глыбока ў маім сэрцы. Так на сьвет зьявілася аднайменная адаптацыя ад Setivir, у якой дзіўным і прыгожым чынам зьмяшаліся журботны, кранальны жаночы вакал, беларускі народны сьпеў і мой улюбёны дабстэп. Гэтая вэрсія, як і арыгінал, гучыць гаротна — яна працятая самотай і жалем расстаньня, — а моцная электронная аснова толькі падкрэсьлівае лірычнасьць твора.

## en

A whole layer of Belarusian history and folk memory is bound up with conscription and military duty. In the old days, young men — some with pride, some without — went off to defend the homeland, while their sweethearts stayed behind, waiting for their beloved to return. It is no accident that recruit songs — songs of seeing young men off to long years of army service — became a genre of Belarusian folklore in their own right. And it is exactly such a story, the parting of a young couple, that is told in the Belarusian folk ballad «Бітая дарожанька» (*Bitaia darozhanka* — "The Well-Trodden Road"; the diminutive *darozhanka* gives the road an affectionate, tender ring).

This song sank into my soul back in my Rakaŭ folk-singing days. It was performed by my older friends from "Hascinets" ("Гасцінец") — the adult folk ensemble of Rakaŭ (as opposed to the children's one, "Vianok"), led by Yelizaveta Stanislavauna Piatrouskaya. I had my share of time in it too — already a teenager, and mostly as a folk dancer (my voice was just breaking, and there was little time left for singing anyway: teenage years, plenty of business outside the choir — well, you know what I mean 😊).

::: cut About the Hascinets ensemble
Since 2011, Hascinets has held the honorary state title of "Merited Amateur Ensemble of the Republic of Belarus." And here is what Professor Mikhail Dryneuski — the longtime director of Belarus's National Academic Folk Choir and a true maître of Belarusian folk song — once said about the group:

> "There are many different folklore ensembles, but such purity, such great truth, such national essence as Hascinets carries — we shall not find anywhere else… Not everyone is capable of passing on a mother's song so responsibly, so purely, with such a sincere soul. I bow low to you…"

![A photo of Hascinets, sometime in 2006–2008. Try to spot me :)](media/9/1.jpg){width=70% align=center}
:::

And then one day in 2008, when I was already studying in Minsk, Yelizaveta Stanislavauna — addressing elders by first name and patronymic is our respectful custom — called me and invited me to take part in a staging of this very song: Hascinets had just received the Diploma of the Special Prize of the President of the Republic of Belarus in the "Amateur Art" category and was to perform at the Palace of the Republic, the country's main state concert hall in Minsk, before the president himself and other high officials. And so, for one evening, I returned to Hascinets: to dance a knight's folk dance on the main stage of the country.

::: cut Watch a recording of that performance
!["Bitaia darozhanka" — Hascinets, 2008](https://youtu.be/PtVhBEfNu-A)
:::

Back then I did not yet fully understand who that "president" was, nor what consequences his "presidency" would bring in 2020 — for the country, and for me personally. But, as the Belarusian saying goes, you cannot throw a word out of a song — and the same holds for history: it was that very performance of "Bitaia darozhanka," with the long, piercing spoken introduction by Yelizaveta Stanislavauna, that stayed deep in my heart. And this is how Setivir's adaptation of the same name came into the world — one in which a mournful, touching female vocal, Belarusian folk singing, and my beloved dubstep came together in a strange and beautiful mixture. Like the original, this version sounds sorrowful — it is steeped in loneliness and the grief of parting — while the powerful electronic foundation only underlines the lyricism of the piece.

::: cut A few notes on the lyrics

- The opening four lines are a declaimed call to arms that precedes the folk text in this arrangement — a different, almost epic register before the lyrical song begins. The "road of struggle" renders *daroha zmaharnaia*: *zmahar* is the Belarusian word for a fighter, one who struggles for freedom.
- **Turaŭ** is one of the oldest cities of Belarus, first mentioned in the chronicles in 980 and once the seat of a medieval principality in the Palessie region. To see one's beloved off "as far as Turaŭ" is to walk beside the departing army for a long, long way.
- The song is built as a call and response: a voice asks the girl why she grieves and guesses she "did not see her beloved off" — and she answers that she did, all the way to Turaŭ.
- The road and the golden saddles "grow dusty" with the same verb in the original (*papylitsia*): it is the dust raised by the marching army, dulling even gold.
- Belarusian folk songs soften even sorrow with affectionate diminutives, and this text is full of them: *darozhanka* (the dear little road), *siodlachki* (the little golden saddles), *parochka* (the young pair), and even *tsiazhanka* — "hard" said tenderly, as if caressing the very pain of parting. Most of this tenderness is untranslatable.
- "We loved one another, and we were in love" renders two different Belarusian verbs: *liubitsia* and *kakhatsia*. Belarusian has a separate word reserved for romantic love — *kakhanne* — and the song insists on both.
- "Not a husband with his wife, nor a brother with his sister" is a classic folk device of negative comparison: the couple's bond is measured against the closest of human ties.

:::
